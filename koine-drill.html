What the terms mean
Case
The ending on a noun that says what job it does in the sentence — subject, direct object, and so on. Greek marks with endings what English marks with word order.
Gender
A fixed property of each noun: masculine, feminine or neuter. It is mostly arbitrary — ἄρτος ‘loaf’ is masculine for no particular reason — so you learn it with the word.
Number
Singular or plural.
Declension
Which family of endings a noun belongs to. 1st declension nouns are usually feminine and end in η or α, 2nd are usually masculine or neuter, 3rd is the varied one. ἡ ὁδός is 2nd declension despite being feminine — the article tells you the gender, the endings tell you the declension.
Person
Who the subject is: the speaker (1st), the one spoken to (2nd), or someone else (3rd).
Tense
Roughly, when: present, future, or past.
Aspect
How the event is viewed, which matters more in Greek than tense does. Perfective sees the whole event with its endpoint — ‘she read the book.’ Imperfective sees it underway or repeated — ‘she was reading’, ‘she used to read.’
Voice
Active: the subject does it. Medio-passive: the subject is affected by it, or undergoes it. Some verbs, like ἔρχομαι, only ever appear medio-passive.
Mood
Indicative states something as real — everything in this drill. The infinitive (‘to eat’) does not.
Augment
The ε- stuck on the front of a past-tense verb: λαμβάνω → ἔλαβον.
Thematic / athematic
Whether a linking vowel sits between stem and ending. κράζ-ο-μεν has one; δίδο-μεν does not, which is why μι verbs look different.
Stem
What is left of a word once you take the ending off. The aorist often uses a different stem from the present, and that is the part you simply memorise.
Luo, Elusa, Efagon, Eluon…
Biblingo's own names for the verb forms, based on shape rather than meaning (from lesson 15.1 on). Traditional labels mislead — “imperfect” implies a contrast with “perfect,” when its real counterpart is the aorist — so each form is instead named after λύω, the verb every Greek grammar uses for paradigms, spelled in English letters to keep it distinct from actual Greek: Luo = present, Luso = future, Elusa = 1st/sigmatic aorist, Efagon = 2nd/thematic aorist (named for ἔφαγον “ate” — kept separate from Elusa since the two aorists share a meaning but not a shape), Eluon = imperfect. “Aorist” still covers Elusa and Efagon together, since it has no competing meaning in English.
Enclitic
A small word with no accent of its own that leans back on the word before it: με, μου, μοι, σε, σου, σοι.
Proclitic
The same idea leaning forward, onto the word after it: ὁ, ἡ, οἱ, αἱ.
Movable nu
An optional ν on the end of certain forms, written here in brackets: κράζουσι(ν), ἐστί(ν), τίσι(ν).
